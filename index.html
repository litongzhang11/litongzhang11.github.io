Research

Job Market Paper

Reference-Dependent Sentiment

Litong Zhang. Sole authored.

Abstract

Under what circumstances is a given paper gain/loss more relevant to trading decisions? I examine whether reference-dependent thinking among investors is associated with the strength of disposition-effect trading. Using 185.1 million StockTwits posts from 2013 to 2024 and an LLM-supervised classification, I construct a daily measure of reference-dependent sentiment, capturing whether investors frame their positions as gains or losses relative to past prices. In the cross-section, talking about gains (losses) is associated with more (less) retail selling of stocks with embedded gains (losses). At the market level, when investors talk more about gains (losses), turnover is higher (lower) on up-market (down-market) days. Around earnings announcements, buying good-news stocks after investors have been talking about gains and selling bad-news stocks after they have been talking about losses earns a Fama-French five-factor alpha of 1.03% per month, or 12.4% annualized, while the reverse strategy does not. Exploiting the introduction of StockTwits’ leaderboard as a plausible shock to framing legitimacy, I find that public reinforcement of gain-loss framing strengthens the relationship between reference-dependent sentiment and trading. Overall, reference-dependent sentiment identifies when the disposition effect shows up more strongly in trading and in prices around earnings news.

Working Papers

Reference-Point Fluency and Memorability

Litong Zhang. Sole authored.

Abstract

Are all reference points equally influential? Round-number purchase prices are associated with a larger disposition effect, the tendency to sell winning investments while holding losing ones. The gain-loss gap in selling is 6.20 percentage points for regular-price positions and 8.60 percentage points for round-price positions, an amplification of 2.40 percentage points (about 39 percent of the regular-price baseline); in account-level regressions the round-versus-regular amplification remains about 1.2 to 2.0 percentage points, or roughly one-third of the baseline disposition effect on regular-price holdings. A stock-split design that rescales purchase bases after the trade addresses purchase-time selection: the amplification persists when the adjusted basis remains round and vanishes when the split breaks roundness. One interpretation is that a round purchase price is a more fluent and memorable reference point. Because it is easier to encode and to retrieve, the distinction between a gain and a loss is more salient when the investor decides whether to sell. The amplification is not explained by taxes, rank-based selling, rebalancing, portfolio concentration, or liquidity, so realization depends on the numerical form of the purchase price and not only on whether a position is at a gain or a loss. A shorter market-level exercise finds that round consensus earnings forecasts are associated with more announcement-window trading and a more complete reaction to positive surprises.

Working in Progress

Reference-Dependent Sentiment and Price Dynamics

Litong Zhang. Sole authored.

Abstract

How does reference-dependent sentiment predict the path of stock prices? I examine whether investors’ discussion of gains and losses predicts returns at different horizons. Using an LLM-supervised classification of StockTwits posts, I measure the daily intensity of reference-dependent sentiment in the market. When investors talk more about gains, subsequent prices fall in the short run, consistent with gain realization creating selling pressure. The effect weakens at intermediate horizons, and prices reverse upward at longer horizons. This pattern is consistent with temporary selling pressure that is gradually absorbed as the market clears, with limits to arbitrage potentially delaying price adjustment. Overall, the evidence suggests that reference-dependent sentiment helps predict not only whether investors trade, but also the timing of price pressure and its reversal.

Inactive Papers (written during my PhD)

Social Gathering and Amplified Familiarity Bias

Litong Zhang. Sole authored.

Abstract

This paper investigates the causal role of contemporaneous social interaction in mutual fund managers’ portfolio choices. Using manually collected information on the Top 30 MBA networks of alumni reunion cohorts, I show that mutual fund managers connected to senior officers (CEO, CFO, and Chairman) at firms increase connected holdings following the MBA alumni reunions. Stock-level performance tests show that fund managers do not achieve significant positive returns on these connected positions after reunion events, favoring the familiarity hypothesis: an increase in contacts leads to a notable surge in buying behavior despite the absence of superior returns. These findings highlight the role of social networks as channels for the formation of behavioral bias.
